30.3285°N, 35.4444°E — Ma'an Governorate

The Petra Water System

Nabataean · 1st c. BCE – 3rd c. CE

Petra lies in a semi-arid region, and its water supply combined springs, cisterns, dams, rock-cut channels, and terracotta pipelines. Water from sources such as Ain Musa was conveyed toward the city through conduits that included the Siq and secondary systems such as Wadi Mataha. Because the system mixed open channels with closed pipelines, two different flow descriptions apply — free-surface flow (Froude number, critical slope) and full-pipe flow (head loss) — and they must not be conflated.

The engineering question is whether the gradients were hydraulically favourable. Ortloff's CFD work suggests that parts of the system are consistent with favourable hydraulic operation — but both key papers share one author, nothing yet validates them independently, and agreement with a modern optimum does not prove design intent: terrain, trial and error, and decades of maintenance feedback could produce the same result.

32.2809°N, 35.8996°E — Jerash (ancient Gerasa)

The Water-Powered Stone Sawmill, Temple of Artemis

Byzantine · 6th century CE

Seigne reported a sixth-century water-powered sawmill for cutting stone in the Temple of Artemis precinct at Jerash, studied in connection with the city's water-supply infrastructure. The mill's existence is documented by excavation.

Machines of this type use a waterwheel to turn a shaft whose crank, through a connecting rod, drives a reciprocating saw frame — a slider-crank mechanism. A third-century relief from Hierapolis, earlier than Jerash, shows the same kind of drive, so Jerash is an example of the slider-crank in use, not an early or first instance. Its technical parameters — crank radius, rod length, wheel size, head, speed, power — are not published, and no efficiency value exists.

Ajloun–Kufranjah valley — Ajloun Governorate

The Mills of Wadi al-Tawaheen

Mamluk–Ottoman periods

The Ajloun–Kufranjah tributaries — Wadi al-Tawaheen, the “Valley of the Mills” — contain a number of watermills, many attributed to the Mamluk and Ottoman periods. Built of local stone, they received water by a channel or tower; the wheel then drove a millstone either directly or through gearing.

For any water-driven wheel, shaft power is P = η·ρ·g·Q·H, and torque is power divided by angular speed — so a speed-increasing gear train trades torque for speed. Levantine mills are commonly described as horizontal wheels on a vertical shaft, fed from a tall tower and grinding without gears, but the wheel type at individual Ajloun mills is not established. Measured heads, discharges, and gear details are not available: this is the weakest-documented of the three cases.

Why this exists

An undergraduate's field notebook, built into an archive.

This project started from a simple frustration: Jordan's engineering history is usually told as archaeology or tourism, rarely as engineering — as a set of design decisions someone had to get right, under real constraints, with the tools of their century.

It's built and maintained by a mechanical engineering undergraduate at Mutah University, as a long-term catalog rather than a finished product. Each site is treated as a case study: what problem it addressed, what the mechanism or system was, what a modern engineer would recognize in it, and how strong the evidence is.

Evidence grades. A — excavated or surveyed evidence plus multiple independent peer-reviewed analyses. B — peer-reviewed, but from a single research programme, or physical evidence without technical parameters. C — mainly non-peer-reviewed or secondary documentation, or an inferred mechanical interpretation.
